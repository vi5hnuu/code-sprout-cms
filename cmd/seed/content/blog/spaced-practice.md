There is a tempting way to prepare for coding interviews: solve as many new problems as possible, as fast as possible. It feels productive. Learning research says it is one of the least efficient ways to remember anything.

## The forgetting curve

Within a few days of solving a problem, most of the details are gone. You remember that you solved it, but not the insight that made it work. Each time you **actively recall** something just as you are starting to forget it, the memory lasts longer before it fades again.

## What spaced practice looks like

1. **Day 0:** solve a problem, or study its solution if you get stuck, and write a one-line note on the key idea.
2. **Day 2–3:** solve it again from a blank editor, without looking at your old code.
3. **Day 7:** solve it once more. If it flows, move it to a monthly review.
4. If you got stuck at any step, restart the cycle for that problem.

The second and third attempts feel slow and a little uncomfortable. That effort is exactly what makes the learning stick.

## Interleave topics

Don't do ten sliding-window problems in a row. Mix in binary search, hashing and dynamic programming. Interleaving forces you to work out **which** technique applies, the skill that random interview questions test.

## Using Code Sprout for it

- Add problems to a personal **list** for each review day.
- Use **notes** on a problem to record the key insight in one sentence.
- Your library's **activity heatmap** shows whether you are practising steadily or in bursts.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Twenty problems you can solve again in your sleep beat a hundred you saw once.
:::
